Business* (Douglas W. Hubbard), concept by concept.

## Foundations: uncertainty and estimation

- **[how-to-measure-anything/01] Why "intangibles" are usually measurable enough for
  decisions** - "immeasurable" almost always decomposes into vaguely-defined, no
  decision attached, and no thought given to how it would show up in observable data;
  answer those three questions and a path to useful measurement appears.
  ([lesson](lessons/01-intangibles-are-measurable.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/02] Measurement as uncertainty reduction, not perfect
  precision** - a measurement is any observation that quantifiably narrows your
  probability range, not an exact number; the "measurement inversion" means the
  variables people most want to measure precisely are often not the highest-value ones
  to measure. ([lesson](lessons/02-measurement-as-uncertainty-reduction.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/03] Calibrated estimation and confidence intervals** - a
  calibrated 90% confidence interval should contain the true value 90% of the time;
  most people are overconfident by default, but the equivalent-bet test and
  anchor-and-reconsider technique reliably train this skill.
  ([lesson](lessons/03-calibrated-estimation.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/04] Decomposition: breaking fuzzy variables into observable
  parts** - break a large, fuzzy quantity into a formula of smaller, more tractable
  sub-quantities and recombine them; independent sub-estimate errors partially cancel,
  producing a better-calibrated total than one big guess.
  ([lesson](lessons/04-decomposition-of-variables.md))

## Measurement design and evidence gathering

- **[how-to-measure-anything/05] Designing useful metrics tied to concrete decisions** -
  a metric only earns its keep if a different measured value would change what someone
  does; work backwards from the decision and its threshold instead of measuring
  whatever is cheap or popular. ([lesson](lessons/05-designing-useful-metrics.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/06] Sampling methods for fast, low-cost evidence
  gathering** - the rule of five (5 random samples bound the population median with
  93.75% confidence) shows small, cheap samples are far more informative than intuition
  suggests, as long as they're genuinely random rather than convenience samples.
  ([lesson](lessons/06-sampling-methods.md))

## Simulation and belief updating

- **[how-to-measure-anything/07] Monte Carlo simulation for decision uncertainty** -
  combine several uncertain input distributions by repeatedly sampling and running them
  through the decision formula thousands of times, producing a full output distribution
  (including the probability of a bad outcome) instead of a single misleading expected
  value. ([lesson](lessons/07-monte-carlo-simulation.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/08] Bayesian updates and integrating new evidence** - a new
  data point should shift your belief by an amount proportional to how strong the new
  evidence is relative to your existing prior; a strong historical baseline should
  barely move in response to one small noisy sample.
  ([lesson](lessons/08-bayesian-updates.md))

## Value of information and organizational decision-making

- **[how-to-measure-anything/09] Value of information and when measurement is worth the
  cost** - Expected Value of (Perfect/Imperfect) Information tells you, before you
  measure, which uncertain variables are actually worth spending time or money to
  narrow down, and which ones to stop worrying about.
  ([lesson](lessons/09-value-of-information.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/10] Quantifying risk and opportunity in portfolio
  decisions** - evaluate roadmap bets as a portfolio, not one at a time; correlated
  risks across initiatives (shared teams, shared fragile dependencies) can make a
  higher-expected-value combination of bets riskier than a diversified,
  slightly-lower-expected-value one. ([lesson](lessons/10-portfolio-risk-and-opportunity.md))
- **[how-to-measure-anything/11] Embedding measurement discipline in organizational
  decision-making** - turning these techniques into a durable organizational habit
  (not a one-off exercise) is mostly a change-management and incentive-design problem:
  pilot for visible proof, train the actual decision-makers, install structural review
  gates, and watch for the cultural tell that it stuck.
  ([lesson](lessons/11-embedding-measurement-discipline.md))
