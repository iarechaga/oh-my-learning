# Managing healthy teams: communication, conflict, and delivery

## TL;DR
A manager's job at the team level is to build systems - communication norms, conflict-resolution habits, and delivery processes - that let the team function well without the manager personally mediating every interaction; a team that only works because the manager is constantly in the room is not actually healthy.

## The idea
Once a manager has the individual-level tools (1:1s, feedback), the next layer of the job is the team as a system, not just a collection of individuals. A team can be full of individually strong, well-managed engineers and still be dysfunctional as a group - unclear communication norms produce duplicated work and surprises, unresolved conflict festers and drives good people out quietly, and a broken or absent delivery process makes the team's actual output unpredictable regardless of how talented anyone is.

Fournier frames the manager's job here as diagnosing which systemic problem is actually present before reaching for a fix - a team that's missing deadlines might have a communication problem (nobody flagged the blocker early), a conflict problem (two people won't collaborate), a scoping problem (the work was underestimated), or a process problem (no one owns tracking status) - and each of those needs a different intervention. Treating a conflict problem as if it were a process problem (adding more status meetings) doesn't fix anything and can make the underlying conflict worse by giving it more airtime without resolving it.

## How it works

### Diagnose communication breakdowns before adding process
When a team keeps getting surprised by problems that should have surfaced earlier, the instinct is often to add more meetings or more reporting. Fournier's advice is to look first at *why* information isn't flowing - is it psychological safety (people are afraid to say "I'm behind" or "I think this is wrong")? Is it a structural gap (no channel or ritual where that information naturally surfaces)? A team with high psychological safety and a lightweight stand-up will surface problems faster than a team with elaborate reporting but a culture of not admitting trouble. Concrete example: a team member silently struggles with a task for a week rather than asking for help, because a past instance of asking for help was met with visible frustration from a teammate. The fix isn't a new status ritual - it's the manager actively normalizing "asking for help early" as a valued behavior, including by publicly thanking people who do it.

### Address conflict directly and early, not by avoidance
Manager-avoidant instincts (hoping two people "work it out themselves," moving one of them to a different project to sidestep the friction) usually let conflict fester and often signal to the rest of the team that the manager won't engage with hard interpersonal problems. Fournier's guidance is to address conflict directly: talk to each party privately first to understand the actual issue (which is often not what it looks like on the surface - a technical disagreement is often a proxy for a trust or respect issue), then, if appropriate, facilitate a direct conversation between them rather than acting purely as a relay. A manager who becomes the sole channel between two conflicting engineers ("tell me what you think and I'll tell them") often prolongs the conflict rather than resolving it, because the two people never actually rebuild the ability to work together directly.

### Build delivery systems the team can run without you
Sprint planning, backlog grooming, clear ownership of tickets, a shared definition of "done" - these are systems that make delivery predictable independent of any single person's memory or heroics. A team that hits deadlines only because the manager personally tracks every task in their head is fragile - it breaks the moment the manager is out sick or focused elsewhere. Fournier's test for a healthy delivery process: could this team function for two weeks with the manager unreachable, and still know what to work on and how to raise a blocker? If not, the manager is the process, not just running one.

### Protect the team from thrash
Frequent reprioritization, unclear ownership between teams, or a firehose of unfiltered requests from stakeholders erodes a team's ability to deliver anything well, even if each individual is skilled. Part of team-level management is buffering the team from this thrash - triaging incoming requests, pushing back on scope changes mid-sprint when the cost is real, and being the one who absorbs some organizational noise so the team can focus. This is a service the manager provides that individual contributors structurally cannot provide for themselves.

## Pros
- A team with healthy communication and conflict-resolution norms surfaces problems early, when they're cheap to fix, instead of late, when they're expensive.
- Systemized delivery processes make output predictable and resilient to any single person (including the manager) being unavailable.
- Directly addressing conflict, rather than avoiding it, tends to retain good people who would otherwise quietly leave a dysfunctional team.

## Cons
- Diagnosing the *real* underlying problem (communication vs. conflict vs. process vs. scoping) takes real observational skill and time, and a wrong diagnosis wastes effort on the wrong fix.
- Direct conflict mediation is emotionally demanding and easy to avoid under time pressure, even though avoidance reliably makes things worse.
- Delivery process that's too heavyweight for the team's size and maturity can itself become the friction it was meant to prevent - process is a tool, not a virtue in itself.

## Alternatives
- **Self-managing/autonomous teams** - some organizations (particularly with strong senior ICs) deliberately minimize manager-driven process, relying on team norms the group sets itself; works well with high trust and experience, less well with junior or newly-formed teams.
- **Scrum/Kanban with a dedicated process owner** - delegate the delivery-process piece to a scrum master or project manager role, freeing the engineering manager to focus more narrowly on people; separates concerns but adds a coordination role and cost.
- **HR/People-Ops-mediated conflict resolution** - for conflicts that involve policy violations, harassment, or issues beyond a manager's scope to resolve directly, escalate to HR rather than attempting direct mediation - knowing this boundary is itself part of managing team health well.

## When to use it
Continuously, as the team's steady-state operating mode - not just when something is visibly broken. Healthy communication and delivery systems are preventive; waiting until conflict or delivery failure is obvious means the cost has already been paid.

## When NOT to use it
Don't add process (more meetings, more reporting, more approval steps) as a default response to a team problem without first diagnosing whether the actual issue is communication, conflict, scoping, or genuine process gaps - mismatched fixes waste the team's time and can mask the real problem longer. Don't personally mediate every interpersonal disagreement indefinitely as a permanent role - the goal is a team that can resolve most friction itself, with the manager as an escalation path, not the default first stop. And don't insert yourself into a conflict you haven't actually diagnosed by talking to both sides privately first - acting only on one person's account of a disagreement is a common and costly mistake.

## Key takeaways / mental model
Ask "would this team still function well for two weeks without me?" - if the honest answer is no, the gap (communication, conflict, or process) is the actual management work in front of you, not whatever is loudest that day.
