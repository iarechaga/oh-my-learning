# Multi-Judge and Debate-Based Evaluation

## TL;DR
A single LLM judge is a single biased estimator: it carries measurable self-preference toward outputs that resemble its own family's style, position bias, verbosity bias, and calibration drift, and none of that is fixed by picking a "better" judge model - it's structural. Multi-judge panels (independent judges, often from different model families, aggregated by vote or weighted average) and debate-based protocols (judges argue their verdicts against each other before a final decision) both trade a single point-estimate for a more expensive but more reliable signal; the staff-level judgment is knowing which failure mode each approach actually fixes, because they are not interchangeable and neither is free.

## The idea
Lesson 03 covered LLM-as-judge design and named its known biases. This lesson is about what to do once you've accepted those biases exist and are structural, not a defect in one particular judge model you could swap out. The core problem: a single judge's verdict is one biased sample from a noisy process, and that's true no matter how carefully the rubric is written or how strong the judge model is, because the biases documented in the research aren't reasoning failures a smarter model fixes - they're systematic tendencies correlated with *what the judge is*, not with how hard it's trying.

Two documented biases make this concrete. Self-preference bias: a judge model measurably favors outputs that resemble its own family's instruction-tuned style, even when scoring blind and even when the content is equivalent - reported at roughly 10-25% uniform bias in one 2026 study, with GPT-4o-as-judge scoring GPT-4o-produced text several points higher than equivalent Claude-produced text on the same rubric. Position bias: a judge scoring two candidate answers side by side shows a preference correlated with *which slot* an answer appears in, independent of its content. Neither bias goes away by using a "better" judge; both are properties of using one judge, once, and trusting the number it returns.

Multi-judge and debate-based evaluation are two different responses to that same root problem, and it matters which one you reach for. A multi-judge panel treats bias as *noise to average out*: independent judges (ideally from different model families, so their biases don't correlate) each score independently, and the panel's verdict is a vote or weighted average. Debate-based evaluation treats disagreement as a *signal to resolve through argument*: judges see each other's verdicts and reasoning, and are asked to reconcile or defend their position before a final call is made - closer to how a human review panel actually works when reviewers disagree, rather than just splitting the difference.

## How it works

### Multi-judge panels: independence as the active ingredient
The mechanism is straightforward - run the same evaluation through N independent judges and aggregate. What makes it work is *independence*, specifically judge diversity across model families: a panel of three GPT-4-class judges from the same family will correlate on the same self-preference bias (all three will over-score GPT-family outputs together), which defeats the purpose of averaging. Practitioner guidance on bias mitigation converges on a specific pattern for high-stakes launch decisions: run a three-judge ensemble spanning three different model families, aggregate by majority vote (for categorical verdicts) or weighted average (for scalar rubric scores), and treat a judge scoring an output from its own family with extra skepticism or exclude that judge from scoring that specific output.

### Worked example: what a panel catches that a single judge misses
A team ships a response-quality rubric, judged by a single instance of Model A. On a batch of 500 responses generated by a mix of Model A and Model B, the single-judge scores show Model A's outputs averaging 0.91 and Model B's averaging 0.84 - read naively, this looks like Model A is the better generator. Re-run the same batch through a three-family panel (Model A, Model B, and a third unrelated Model C, majority vote per case): the gap nearly closes, to 0.88 vs 0.87. The single-judge result wasn't measuring generation quality at all in the 7-point portion of that gap - it was measuring Model A's self-preference for its own family's outputs, and the team would have shipped the wrong model-selection decision on the strength of a single biased estimator, formatted to look like a precise number.

### Debate-based evaluation: disagreement as signal, not noise
Where a panel averages disagreement away, a debate protocol uses disagreement productively. The pattern from 2026 debate-judge research: a small set of agents (in one framework, a critic, a defender, and a judge) argue a specific verdict through structured rounds, each grounded in shared evaluation criteria, before a final decision is rendered - closer to adversarial cross-examination than independent scoring. The "Multi-Agent Debate for LLM Judges with Adaptive Stability Detection" work (NeurIPS 2025) formalizes why this can outperform a static ensemble: debate lets agents iteratively refine a judgment in response to a counter-argument, which a one-shot panel vote structurally cannot do - a panel of three judges who each independently miss the same subtle flaw in a response will simply out-vote the (if any) judge who caught it; a debate protocol gives that judge a chance to argue the flaw explicitly and potentially change the others' verdicts.

### Worked example: debate catching what independent scoring can't
Consider a subtle factual error buried in an otherwise well-written, confident-sounding response - the kind of error a rubric-scoring judge skimming for tone and structure can plausibly miss, because the response *reads* correct. Three independent judges scoring this response in a panel might all rate it highly for the same superficial reason (fluent, well-organized, confident phrasing) - independence doesn't help when the bias is shared across judges rather than idiosyncratic to one. A debate protocol assigns one agent the explicit role of critic, whose job is to argue for flaws rather than score holistically; if the critic surfaces the factual error and can point to it concretely, the defender and judge roles have to engage with that specific claim rather than simply re-averaging their initial impressions - closer to how a factual error survives a panel of casual reviewers but gets caught by one dedicated fact-checker whose entire job is to look for exactly that.

### Cost, and why "a small number of debate rounds" is the load-bearing finding
Both approaches multiply judge-call cost over a single judge - a three-judge panel is roughly 3x the judge calls of a single judge, and a debate protocol adds further rounds on top of that. This is real money at production scale, not a rounding error, and it's the reason neither approach is a default-on setting for every evaluation. The 2026 multi-agent debate safety-evaluation research (arXiv:2511.06396) reports two findings that matter for the cost trade-off specifically: the debate framework, using smaller backbone models in a structured critic/defender/judge protocol, improved reliability over both matched-size single-judge baselines and prior multi-agent judge designs, while remaining more economical than routing every evaluation through a frontier-tier single judge (GPT-4o, in that study's pricing snapshot) - and, critically, a small number of debate rounds captured most of the achievable gain, meaning the cost multiplier from debate doesn't need to scale with rounds run to convergence; two or three rounds get most of the benefit that ten rounds would.

### Choosing between panel, debate, and single judge - a decision, not a default
- **Single judge**: acceptable for low-stakes, high-volume, exploratory evaluation (development-time iteration, a sampled slice of online evaluation per lesson 05) where the cost of an occasional biased verdict is low and getting a fast, cheap signal matters more than precision.
- **Multi-judge panel**: reach for this when the decision is consequential enough that a single biased estimator's error margin matters (model-selection decisions, a regression-suite gate per lesson 06 that blocks a release) and the failure mode you're worried about is *systematic bias from one judge's tendencies* - self-preference, family-correlated scoring, calibration drift.
- **Debate**: reach for this when the failure mode is specifically *subtle errors that fluent, confident-sounding output can hide from a holistic-scoring judge* - safety evaluation, factual-correctness checks, adversarial or jailbreak-style inputs - where getting judges to explicitly argue a specific claim surfaces something independent scoring structurally can't.

These aren't mutually exclusive tiers to pick exactly one from forever; a mature evaluation stack routes cheap single-judge scoring to the bulk of traffic, escalates disagreement or low-confidence cases to a multi-judge panel, and reserves debate for the highest-stakes subset (safety-critical verdicts, launch-gating decisions) where the extra rounds are worth the cost.

## Pros
- **Multi-judge panels average out judge-specific, uncorrelated bias**, and with cross-family diversity specifically counter self-preference bias that no single judge, however capable, can self-correct.
- **Debate protocols surface disagreement productively** rather than diluting it into an average, catching subtle flaws a fluent response can hide from holistic scoring.
- **Both produce a more defensible verdict for consequential decisions** - a launch gate or regression-suite block backed by a three-family panel vote is harder to dismiss as "just one model's opinion" than a single-judge score.
- **Debate cost is bounded in practice** - reported findings show most of the reliability gain comes from a small number of rounds, not open-ended argument to convergence.

## Cons
- **Cost multiplies** - roughly N times the judge calls for an N-judge panel, plus additional rounds for debate; neither is a free upgrade over a single judge.
- **Panels don't help against a bias shared across all panel members** - if every available judge model exhibits the same bias (e.g., all judges under-penalize confident, fluent phrasing regardless of accuracy), voting or averaging doesn't cancel a bias that isn't idiosyncratic to one judge.
- **Debate introduces new failure modes of its own** - a persuasive but wrong argument from one agent can sway others (a documented risk in multi-agent debate research generally: debate amplifies correctness when the correct position is defensible, but can also amplify a confidently-argued wrong position if the debate protocol isn't carefully designed), and naive aggregation (plain majority vote) has been shown to fail even when individual judges independently reach the correct verdict.
- **More moving parts to calibrate** - a single judge needs one calibration pass against human labels (lesson 03); a panel or debate protocol needs its aggregation rule, judge-family selection, and round count all calibrated together, which is a larger and less well-trodden design space.

## Alternatives
- **Single judge with careful bias-aware prompting** - cheaper, and can partially mitigate known biases (asking the judge to ignore length, randomizing candidate order to counter position bias) without the cost multiplier of a panel. Doesn't touch self-preference bias, which is structural to using that judge's own family at all.
- **Human review for the highest-stakes subset instead of debate** - for a small enough volume of the most consequential verdicts, routing to a human reviewer sidesteps LLM-judge bias entirely at the cost of latency and reviewer time; often the right choice when the volume is genuinely small and the stakes genuinely high, rather than building debate infrastructure for a handful of cases a month.
- **Reference-based scoring where ground truth exists** - if a verifiable answer or automated check (a test suite passing, a schema validating) is available, it sidesteps judge bias entirely because there's no judgment call to bias; only applicable where such a check exists, which trajectory- and rubric-based evaluation (lessons 03-04) exists specifically to handle when it doesn't.

## When to use it
Reach for a multi-judge panel when a decision is consequential enough that a single judge's documented biases (self-preference, position, calibration drift) could plausibly flip the outcome - model-selection decisions, regression-suite release gates, any verdict that will be defended to stakeholders. Reach for debate specifically when the risk is a fluent-but-wrong output slipping past holistic single- or multi-judge scoring - safety evaluation, factual-accuracy-critical domains, adversarial-input handling - and the extra cost of a few debate rounds is justified by how expensive a missed error would be.

## When NOT to use it
Do not default to a multi-judge panel or debate for high-volume, low-stakes, exploratory evaluation - development-time iteration on a prompt, or the bulk of a sampled online-evaluation stream (lesson 05) - where a single judge's occasional bias is an acceptable cost against the multiplied expense of running every case through three judges or several debate rounds; escalate only the disagreement or low-confidence cases. Do not treat a multi-judge panel as a fix for a bias every available judge shares - if the bias is common across model families (not just self-preference within one family), no amount of voting among biased judges converges on an unbiased answer, and the fix is a different evaluation method (reference-based scoring, human review) rather than more judges of the same kind.

## Key takeaways / mental model
A single LLM judge is one biased sample, and the bias is structural - tied to what the judge *is* (its family, its tendency to reward fluent phrasing, its position sensitivity), not to how well it's prompted. A multi-judge panel treats that bias as noise to average out, and works specifically when judges are independent enough (different families) that their biases don't correlate. A debate protocol treats disagreement as a signal to resolve through argument, and works specifically when the failure mode is a subtle flaw that holistic scoring - by one judge or several voting independently - can miss because the output simply reads well. Neither is a universal upgrade over a single judge; both cost real money at scale, and the staff-level skill is routing cheap single-judge scoring to the bulk of cases and reserving the multiplied cost of panels and debate for the decisions where a biased or superficially-fooled verdict would actually hurt.

## Self-check questions
1. A three-judge panel, all instances of the same model family with different prompts, is used to reduce judge bias on a launch decision. Explain why this panel design does not actually address self-preference bias, and what would need to change.
2. A team observes that their multi-judge panel and their single-judge baseline agree on 96% of cases and want to conclude the panel was unnecessary complexity. What's missing from that conclusion - what should they check about the 4% disagreement before deciding the panel added no value?
3. Why does debate-based evaluation catch a fluent-but-factually-wrong response better than a panel of three independently-scoring judges might, even though the panel has more total judge opinions? What's the structural difference?
4. The arXiv:2511.06396 debate-safety-evaluation research found that "a small number of debate rounds is sufficient to capture most of the gain." Why does this finding matter for whether debate-based evaluation is practical to run at production scale, versus academically interesting but too expensive to deploy?
5. You're designing evaluation for a customer-facing agent that (a) gets rubric-scored on every response as part of online evaluation, and (b) occasionally handles safety-sensitive requests that get flagged for stricter review. Sketch which evaluation tier (single judge, multi-judge panel, debate) you'd assign to each path, and justify the cost/rigor trade-off for each.
